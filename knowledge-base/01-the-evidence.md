# 1. Stuttering in school age children — what the evidence actually says

Compiled September 2026. Every claim here is traceable to a source listed at the bottom.

---

## The basics

**What it is.** Speech interrupted by unplanned repetitions, prolongations and blocks. Speech Pathology Australia describes it as "unplanned breaks, repetitions and stretches of sounds and words".

**How common.** About 1 in 100 people stutter. Incidence in preschoolers is much higher — around 11% of children stutter at some point.

**What causes it.** Unknown, but it is neurological, not psychological. It is **not** caused by anxiety, tiredness, a specific event, parenting style, or any psychological problem. This matters: parents routinely blame themselves, and the evidence does not support that.

**When it starts.** Typically ages 2 to 4, during rapid language development.

**Natural recovery.** Roughly two thirds of preschool cases resolve without treatment. Critically, **nobody can predict which children will recover.** Waiting is a gamble, not a strategy.

---

## Why school age is different from preschool

This is the single most important thing to understand, and the thing most parent-facing material glosses over.

By the time a child is at school, the odds have shifted. Natural recovery is far less likely — the two-thirds figure applies to preschoolers, not to a 9 year old who has been stuttering for five years. The research base also thins out dramatically:

> "No high-level evidence for interventions with school-aged children or adolescents."
> — Brignell et al., systematic review and meta-analysis, 2021

That is not the same as "nothing works". It means the randomised controlled trials that exist were mostly done with preschoolers, and school age programs are largely adaptations of preschool ones with more variable results. Treatment for a school age child is legitimately harder and slower, and any provider who promises a cure is overselling.

What also changes at school age:

- **The child now has opinions about their own speech.** Avoidance, word substitution, and choosing not to speak start showing up. These are invisible on a fluency count but are the more damaging problem.
- **Social consequences arrive.** See §5 on bullying — the numbers are grim.
- **Complete fluency stops being the only sensible goal.** Confident, willing communication matters at least as much as smooth speech, and for many children more.

The ASRC notes that children can develop anxiety about speaking **even by age 7**.

---

## Psychosocial impact — the part that is easy to underestimate

- Bullying incidence among children who stutter reaches **up to 81%**, against a general schoolchild bullying rate of 49–58%. The classroom is the main site.
- Children who stutter show elevated anxiety and depression symptoms, higher social isolation and more peer rejection.
- The effects persist: adults who were bullied as children who stutter show higher social anxiety, greater fear of negative evaluation, lower self esteem and reduced life satisfaction.
- Family mental health history significantly predicts anxiety and depression scores in these children.

**Practical implication.** A treatment plan that only counts stutters and ignores how the child feels about speaking is treating half the problem. Ask any prospective clinician how they address the emotional and social side. If they have no answer, keep looking.

---

## Risk factors considered when assessing persistence

Clinicians weigh: stuttering frequency, severity, typology, time since onset, family history of stuttering, communication impact, and the child's own frustration or anxiety.

Caveat straight from the literature: **prognostic indicators have not been clearly established.** Pretreatment severity is the only known predictor of treatment *duration*.

---

## Co-occurring conditions

Stuttering is more likely alongside other communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder, and Down syndrome. Research trials routinely exclude these children, so **the evidence base for treating a child with a concomitant diagnosis is largely absent**. If that applies, expect a clinician to be improvising from principles rather than following a protocol, and expect them to say so honestly.

---

## Timing

For a mild, non-distressing preschool stutter, clinicians often monitor for six months post onset; delaying up to 12 months does not appear to compromise treatment duration. **This grace period does not apply to school age children.** The ASRC position is to start as soon as possible.

---

## What the research does not support

- **Telling a child to slow down or relax.** No evidence, and it teaches the child that their speech is a problem to be managed for the listener's comfort.
- **Waiting for it to be grown out of** once the child is at school.
- **Apps as a replacement for therapy.** See §7. Digital tools have essentially no high-quality efficacy evidence for children. They are practice scaffolding, adherence support and measurement — nothing more.
- **A single technique for everyone.** Response to treatment in this age group is genuinely variable.
