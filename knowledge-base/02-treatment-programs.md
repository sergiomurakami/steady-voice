# 2. The treatment programs, compared

Every named program a clinician might propose, what it actually involves, and how strong the evidence is.

---

## Quick comparison

| Program | Best fit age | Delivered by | Evidence | Available in AU |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lidcombe Program | 3–6 primarily; adapted 6–12 | Parent, clinician-supervised | Strongest — multiple RCTs | Yes, developed here |
| RESTART-DCM | Preschool | Parent + clinician | RCT, non-inferior to Lidcombe | Training only in Netherlands |
| Westmead Program | Very young children | Parent | Promising, needs more research | Yes (AU/NZ) |
| **Oakville Program** | **School age** | **Parent + clinician** | **Phase II: 77% severity drop at 12mo** | **Yes, developed here** |
| Camperdown Program | Adolescents and adults | Clinician + self-managed | Trials show reduction, variable | Yes |
| Palin PCI | Preschool | Parent | Small well-designed studies | Training mainly UK |
| Palin STSC (8–14) | 8–14 | Clinician + family | Feasibility RCT completed | Training mainly UK |
| Van Riper stuttering modification | School age up | Clinician | RCT evidence 2023 | Widely taught |

## Lidcombe Program

**The one with the strongest evidence.** Developed in Australia. Two independent replicated Phase III RCTs.

**How it works.** The parent, not the clinician, delivers treatment. The parent gives *verbal contingencies* — brief spoken responses — to the child's speech during everyday life and during short daily practice sessions.

**Structure.**

- **Stage 1** — weekly 45–60 minute clinic visits where the clinician trains and supervises the parent. Goal: no stuttering or almost none.
- **Stage 2** — maintenance. Visits at widening intervals: 2, 4, 8, then 16 weeks. Contingencies are systematically withdrawn. Typically 30 minute visits.

**Daily practice.** Once or twice a day, 10–15 minutes, quiet setting, with a book or a game.

**The five verbal contingencies.**

For stutter-free speech (used far more often):

1. **Praise** — "that was lovely smooth talking", "good talking, no bumps"
2. **Request self-evaluation** — "was that smooth?", "were there any bumps there?"
3. **Acknowledge** — "that was smooth", "no bumpy words"

For unambiguous stuttering (used sparingly):

4. **Acknowledge** — "that was bumpy", "that was a stuck word"
5. **Request self-correction** — "can you say it again?", "see if you can say that without the bump"

**Progression to Stage 2.** For three consecutive weekly visits: parent severity ratings of 0–1 with at least four days at 0, and clinician ratings of 0–1.

**Duration.** Median around 16 clinic visits. 10% take more than 26 weeks. Pretreatment severity predicts duration.

**Cautions, verbatim from the treatment guide:**

> "It is a rule of thumb that there should be far more verbal contingencies for stutter-free speech than for stuttered speech."

> "If a child reacts in any way negatively to requests for self correction, it is essential that they be stopped immediately."

> "It is essential that a professionally qualified clinician trains, guides, and supervises parents. The treatment is not designed for administration by parents independently of clinicians."

> "Delayed and inaccurate verbal contingencies are unlikely to be effective."

**Read that third one twice.** Do not run Lidcombe from a book or an app. The app in this project supports a clinician-guided program; it does not substitute for one.

**School age.** Phase II telehealth trials for 6–12 year olds exist and show it can be adapted, with more variable responsiveness than in preschoolers. Telehealth delivery is as effective as in-clinic.

**Failure mode.** Parents get complacent after Stage 1 success and drop the maintenance schedule. Relapse follows. Contingencies for stutter-free speech must continue through Stage 2.

## RESTART-DCM

Dutch. Based on the Demands and Capacities Model — stuttering emerges when environmental demands outstrip the child's current capacity for fluent speech.

Modifies the environment across linguistic, motoric, emotional and cognitive domains. A 199-participant RCT against Lidcombe found **little difference in outcomes at 18 months**. Training is confined to the Netherlands, so an Australian clinician offering "RESTART" is likely offering DCM-informed practice rather than the certified program.

---

## Westmead Program and Oakville Program (syllable timed speech)

**Westmead** is the preschool version; **Oakville** is the school age one, and it is the program to ask about by name for a 6–12 year old in Australia. For a child who bumps frequently in every context and is not anxious about it, this is the best-matched treatment available — see §8.

### What syllable timed speech actually is

Saying each syllable in time to a rhythmic beat: *"The chil-dren are play-ing on the tram-po-line."*

- Every syllable **evenly stressed** and roughly the same duration
- Syllables **joined smoothly to the next — legato**, not separated
- **Normal volume, pitch and intonation** maintained throughout
- Taught slow first, worked up toward near-normal speed

The treatment guide is emphatic that it must never sound unnatural or staccato. If it sounds chopped up, the beat is too fast. Calling it "robotic" is a misdescription and leads people to do it wrong.

It is explicitly **temporary** — a training wheel that gets removed. Saying that to the child aids compliance.

### Oakville structure

**Early Stage 1.** Weekly ~45 minute clinician consultations. Parent and child learn STS by demonstration, imitation and practice. Home practice **four to six times a day, five to ten minutes each**. The parent speaks in STS too — it is a duet, not a test. Consultations drop to fortnightly once the child holds STS consistently and complies with the schedule.

**Later Stage 1 — hybrid sessions.** Once severity drops outside practice, contingencies come in and sessions become **half STS, half natural speech**, about three a day at 10–15 minutes. Fluency generalises from the first half into the second. The ratio then shifts progressively toward natural — 50%, 60%, 70% — with the aim of eliminating STS practice altogether. If bumps climb in the natural half, revert to 70–80% STS.

**Moving to Stage 2.** For two consecutive fortnightly consultations: clinician severity rating 0 or 1, and daily parent ratings of 0–1 for the preceding week with at least four days at 0.

**Stage 2.** ~30 minute consultations at widening intervals — four at 4 weeks, two at 8 weeks, then one or two at 12 weeks — typically until minimal stuttering has held for around a year. Parents learn to reintroduce a little practice themselves if severity creeps back to 1 or 2.

### Contingencies (later Stage 1 onward)

For **stutter-free speech**: "that was great talking", "that was smooth", or non-verbal — a high five, a token. Genuine, varied, in the parent's own style. Used far more than the other kind.

For **stuttered speech**: usually a request to say the word again in STS. Sparingly, never punitive, and **only on unambiguous stuttering** — the guide is explicit that if the parent has any doubt about whether a disfluency was a real stutter, they should not use a contingency at all.

Clinicians teach the positive contingency first, so the child gets comfortable with feedback before anything corrective appears.

### Transfer into everyday conversation

Once STS holds through a whole practice session, gentle prompts appear outside practice:

- **No more than about one prompt per hour**, and only if the child tolerates them
- Only in **comfortable settings** — family, not in front of peers
- The parent can model by using STS themselves and letting the child join
- **Never as a response to the child's stuttering.** A prompt that lands right after a bump turns the technique into a telling-off

Everyday conversations are **never modified** to engineer fluency — the parent takes advantage of naturally occurring stutter-free periods instead. Eventually feedback during everyday conversation replaces feedback during practice sessions entirely, though the guide warns that withdrawing practice sessions should be done extremely cautiously.

### Warnings from the guide

> "It is essential that a clinician trains, guides, and supervises the parent during the Oakville Program. The treatment is not designed to be administered by the parent independent of this specialist support."

> "A parent delivering contingencies incorrectly is a common reason why children do not progress as expected through Stage 1."

Also flagged: over-correction; letting practice become boring or repetitive; complacency after Stage 1 producing relapse; and the fact that a child's speech in the clinic often does not match their speech at home.

### Severity rating

Oakville uses **0–10** (0 = none, 1 = extremely mild, 10 = extremely severe) — note this differs from Lidcombe's 0–9. Daily parent ratings covering the majority of the day, plus clinician ratings at each consultation. The guide explicitly permits **ratings for specific situations** (meals, shopping and so on) as a variation, and notes that a recording of a few minutes of everyday conversation gives "a much more realistic measure" than recall. Children from about **age 9** can be trained to rate themselves alongside the parent, which surfaces school and peer information a parent never sees.

### Evidence

Two trials, as at the 2023 guide.

- **Preschool** — three-arm RCT, 91 children, comparing Oakville, Westmead and Lidcombe. No difference in %SS between groups. **Drop-out around 43%** for the STS treatments — the schedule is demanding, and that is the known weakness of this family of programs.
- **School age** — non-randomised trial, 22 children aged 6–11, 19 completing. **Group mean severity reduction of 77% at 12 months** post-treatment (82% excluding the two least responsive). Considerable individual variation; many participants had co-occurring speech and language problems.

Encouraging rather than conclusive. This is Phase II work, not a randomised controlled trial.

**Comorbidity note.** Children with additional issues — ADHD, developmental delay, autism — typically take longer and may need the imitation stage for much longer.

**Bilingual note.** If a child stutters more in another language than the treatment language, the guide says treatment should also be done in that other language.

## Camperdown Program

Speech restructuring using prolonged speech. Developed by the ASRC. Aimed at adolescents and adults; trials with 12–17 year olds, mostly by webcam, showed overall severity reductions with variable efficiency.

Relevant for a child approaching high school age.

---

## Palin approaches (Michael Palin Centre, London)

**Palin Parent-Child Interaction (PCI)** — preschool. Parents do targeted 15 minute sessions several times a week, modifying their own interaction style: slower speech rate, following the child's lead, more praise, comments rather than questions. May also address behaviour management, sleep and family routines.

**Palin STSC (8–14)** — the school age program, and one of the better structured options for this exact age band.

- A block of **10 weekly one-hour sessions**, then reviews through the year.
- Both parents attend where possible. It is family therapy, not child-only therapy.
- Three aims: communicate more confidently and participate more; reduce anxiety and worry about stammering; maximise fluency and reduce struggle.
- Adapted from their two-week group programme.
- Can be delivered remotely.

A feasibility RCT comparing Palin STSC(8–14) with usual treatment has been run. Note the ordering of their aims — confidence and anxiety come before fluency. That ordering is deliberate and well matched to what §1 says about school age children.

## Van Riper stuttering modification

Not a branded program — a therapeutic tradition. The goal is not to eliminate stuttering but to stutter more easily and with less struggle and avoidance.

Four phases:

1. **Identification** — the child learns to notice and describe their own moments of stuttering without shame.
2. **Desensitisation** — deliberately stuttering on purpose (voluntary stuttering), reducing the fear response.
3. **Modification** — three techniques:
   - **Cancellation** — finish the stuttered word, pause, then say it again easily.
   - **Pull-out** — during a block, ease out of it mid-word rather than forcing through.
   - **Preparatory set** — anticipate a hard word and enter it gently.
4. **Stabilisation** — generalise to real life.

A 2023 randomised clinical trial in the *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* tested stuttering modification treatment specifically in school age children — one of the few high-quality school age trials in existence.

**Why this matters for a school age child.** Fluency shaping alone can produce a child who is fluent in the clinic and silent in the classroom. Modification directly targets avoidance and fear, which is the thing that follows a person into adulthood.

## Fluency shaping techniques (the toolkit)

These get taught across programs and are what most practice apps drill:

- **Easy onset** — start a word with gentle, gradual voicing rather than a hard attack. Practised first on vowel-initial words.
- **Light articulatory contact** — touch the lips, tongue and teeth lightly on plosives (p, b, t, d, k, g) rather than pressing hard.
- **Prolonged / stretchy speech** — stretch vowels, ease into all sounds, slow the overall rate.
- **Pausing and phrasing** — deliberate pauses at natural phrase boundaries; reduces the pressure of long utterances.
- **Regulated breathing** — speech happens on the exhalation. Taught as: how breathing works → passive breathing exercises → single words on a normal exhale → phrases chunked to the breath.
- **GILCU (Gradual Increase in Length and Complexity of Utterance)** — one fluent word, then two, then three, laddering up to five minutes of fluent conversation.
- **Time-out / self-imposed time-out** — a signalled brief pause after a moment of stuttering. Behavioural contingency approach; success varies by individual.

**Honest caveat.** These techniques produce clinic fluency reliably. Transfer to the playground is the hard part, and it is where programs succeed or fail.
