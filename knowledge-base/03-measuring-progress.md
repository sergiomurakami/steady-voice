# 3. Measuring progress

What to record, how, and what the numbers actually mean. This section is the specification behind the parent tracking in the app.

---

## The Severity Rating (SR) scale — the primary daily measure

The measure used in the Lidcombe Program and the one most Australian clinicians will ask a parent to keep.

**Scale: 0 to 9.**

- **0** = no stuttering
- **1** = extremely mild stuttering
- **9** = extremely severe stuttering

**How it is used.**

- The parent assigns **one rating per day**, reflecting the child's typical speech across that whole day — not a single sample, not the worst moment.
- The clinician assigns their own rating at each weekly visit.
- Agreement is considered acceptable when the parent rating is **within one scale value of, or identical to**, the clinician rating.
- Parents are trained on the scale at the first visit by rating speech samples after the clinician explains the endpoints.

**Why it works.** Research confirms parents rate accurately once trained. Self-reported and parent-reported severity has been shown to be accurate enough for large-scale data collection.

**What it is used for.** Tracking progress, giving parent and clinician a shared language, and guiding when to deliver verbal contingencies.

**Anchoring your own scale.** The numbers only mean something if your use of them is consistent. Set your own anchors in the first week and write them down — for example, note what a 2 day and a 6 day look like in your house — then rate against those anchors rather than against how the day felt emotionally. A bad day at school makes a parent rate high; the scale is about speech, not about the day.

---

## Percentage syllables stuttered (%SS)

The objective clinical measure. Clinicians count stuttered syllables in a speech sample and divide by total syllables.

**Realistic expectation for a parent.** Full %SS counting requires training and recorded samples. Approximating it at home is possible but noisy. The app includes a simple tap-counter for a timed sample — treat the output as a rough trend line, not a clinical figure, and never argue with a clinician's count using it.

---

## Beyond fluency counts

Fluency measures miss the thing that matters most for a school age child. These are the validated instruments a good clinician will also use:

**OASES** — *Overall Assessment of the Speaker's Experience of Stuttering.* Measures impact on the person's life: general perceptions, reactions to stuttering, communication in daily situations, quality of life. There is a school age version (OASES-S). Ask for it.

**Palin Parent Rating Scales (Palin PRS)** — captures the parent's perspective across three factors: impact of stuttering on the child, severity and impact on the parent, and parental confidence and knowledge. Useful precisely because it measures the parent as well.

**KiddyCAT** — communication attitude test for preschool and early school age children. Measures how the child feels about their own speech.

**CALMS** — a five-factor assessment (Cognitive, Affective, Linguistic, Motor, Social) for school age children. Comprehensive, and matches the multidimensional reality.

**SSI-4** — Stuttering Severity Instrument, 4th edition. Clinician-administered scoring of frequency, duration and physical concomitants.

---

## What to track at home, in priority order

1. **Daily SR (0–9)** — the backbone. One number, every day, takes five seconds.
2. **Whether practice happened** and for how long. Adherence is the single biggest predictor of whether a home program works, and the documented failure point of the Westmead program.
3. **Avoidance events** — did the child not put their hand up, swap a word, ask you to order for them, avoid the phone? These are the school age markers that fluency counts miss entirely.
4. **Situation notes** — where and when it was hardest. Tiredness, excitement, competing for a turn, phone calls, reading aloud, new people.
5. **Wins** — a hard word said easily, a hand raised, a question asked of a stranger. Track these deliberately; they are what a child can see.

---

## Reading the chart honestly

**Stuttering is naturally variable.** Day-to-day and week-to-week swings are normal and expected. A bad day after a good week is not relapse and does not mean the treatment failed.

Look at the **7-day rolling average**, not individual days. Look for the direction of the trend over a month. Take the chart to appointments — a clinician reading four weeks of daily ratings gets more signal than they get from thirty minutes of observing your child in an unfamiliar room.

**Do not let the child see you being disappointed by the number.** If the rating becomes a score the child is graded on, you have created exactly the performance anxiety the treatment is meant to reduce. Rate after they are in bed.
