# 4. School, teasing and the social side

The part that follows a child into adulthood. Treat it as at least as important as fluency work.

---

## The numbers

- Bullying incidence among schoolchildren who stutter: **up to 81%**. General schoolchild bullying rate: 49–58%.
- The **classroom** is the primary site — not the playground. Nearly half of surveyed students reported name-calling, mockery and physical aggression.
- Adults who were bullied as children who stutter show higher social anxiety, greater fear of negative evaluation, lower self esteem and lower life satisfaction.

Teacher knowledge about stuttering has been measured as "insufficient" — but training programs measurably improve it. So the conversation with the teacher is worth having, and it works.

---

## What to ask the school for

Take this list to the teacher at the start of the year, or now.

**Classroom participation**

- Agree expectations individually, with input from the child and the speech pathologist. Do not assume the child wants to be excused — many don't.
- Call on the child **early** in a round rather than late. Waiting builds anxiety.
- Ask questions that need **few words** at first.
- Tell the whole class there is time, and that quick answers are not required. Frame it as a class norm, not an accommodation for one child.

**Reading aloud**

- Use **paired or unison reading**. Most children who stutter are fluent when reading in unison with someone else. Build from there toward solo reading.
- Let the child read a passage they have seen beforehand.

**Oral presentations**

- Options to negotiate: present to the teacher alone, present to a small group, pre-record a video, present with a partner, or use more visual aids with less speaking.
- The goal is participation with dignity, not exemption. Exemption teaches avoidance.

**Assessment**

- Do not mark down for dysfluency in oral assessment.
- Allow extra time where a task is timed and spoken.

**Everyday teacher behaviour**

- Speak in an unhurried way, pausing frequently. Modelling beats instructing.
- Do **not** finish the child's words or speak for them.
- Do **not** say "slow down", "relax", "take a breath", "think about what you want to say".
- Keep normal eye contact and wait. Show you are listening to **what** was said, not **how**.
- Hold the same academic standards as for every other student.

**Teasing**

- Talk to the child privately about how they want it handled.
- Address anyone bullying privately: teasing is unacceptable, full stop.
- Escalate to the guidance officer if it persists.
- Evidence supports **role-play and self-disclosure** as effective — a child who can say "I stutter, sometimes it takes me a minute" out loud, calmly, is markedly less of a target. Practise the sentence at home until it is boring.

## What parents should and should not do at home

**Do**

- Slow your own speech and pause more. Model it; do not instruct it.
- Give the child your full attention when they speak — stop, turn, look.
- Wait. Let them finish. The silence is more comfortable for them than being rescued.
- Reduce time pressure and competition for turns at the dinner table.
- Talk about stuttering openly and matter-of-factly. Naming it is not making it worse.
- Praise brave speaking, not just smooth speaking. "You asked the waiter yourself, that was great" beats "that was smooth".
- Keep one calm, unhurried, one-on-one talking time daily.

**Do not**

- Say "slow down", "relax", "take a deep breath", "start again", "think first".
- Finish words or sentences.
- Tell them to stop and start over in front of others.
- Show visible frustration or look away.
- Make fluency a condition of getting what they asked for.
- Treat a bad speech day as a behaviour problem.

**On self-blame.** Stuttering is not caused by parenting style, anxiety, or any psychological problem. Speech Pathology Australia states this explicitly. Nothing you did caused it.

---

## Talking to the child about it

Age-appropriate framing that holds up:

- "Your brain and your mouth are learning to work together on a really fast job. Sometimes they get out of sync. That's called stuttering, and lots of people have it."
- "Stuttering isn't something wrong with you. It's not because you're nervous or not trying hard enough."
- "Some days will be bumpier than others. That's normal and it doesn't mean anything went wrong."
- "What you have to say is worth waiting for."

**Voluntary stuttering as a family game.** Deliberately bumping on words yourself, casually and without ceremony, is a genuine desensitisation technique from the Van Riper tradition. It removes the taboo faster than any conversation.
