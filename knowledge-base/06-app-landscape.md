# 6. The app landscape — what exists and what it misses

Reviewed September 2026.

---

## Evidence health warning, up front

There is essentially **no high-quality efficacy evidence for stuttering apps in children.** The published work amounts to pilot validation studies. Every app in this space markets on technique names borrowed from programs whose evidence was generated by clinician-delivered therapy, not by an app.

That does not make them worthless. It makes their honest job narrower: **practice scaffolding, adherence support, measurement, and lowering the emotional temperature around practice.** An app that claims more than that is lying.

This is the design constraint for anything built here.

---

## Stutter Stars (SAY IT Labs, Leuven, Belgium)

The closest reference point for a child-facing app.

- **Ages:** built for children, roughly 5–12, App Store rated 3+
- **Platforms:** iOS, iPad, Android. Limited desktop.
- **Model:** an AI-powered, voice-controlled video game. Islands, each teaching a different technique. A character-driven adventure.
- **Techniques covered:** easy onset, light contact, pitch variation, rate of speech, loudness changes, beat practice (activating the brain's timing system), mindfulness/breath work and sustained phonation, prosody.
- **Approaches claimed:** fluency shaping, fluency modification, mindfulness, prosody.
- **Feedback:** speech recognition on actual voice, immediate feedback, automatic data collection into progress graphs, reports shareable with parents or therapists.
- **Philosophy:** explicitly supports people who stutter without trying to "fix" them, while offering fluency techniques to those who want them. Good positioning.
- **Pricing:** free tier plus subscription.
- **Weaknesses reported:** requires consistent practice; users have reported trouble accessing paid content after subscribing.

**What it gets right:** gamification that a child will actually open, technique breadth, non-pathologising philosophy.

## Stamurai

- **Target:** adults. This is the key point — it is repeatedly described as having a complex interface and overwhelming content volume for young children.
- **Techniques:** fluency shaping, stuttering modification, CBT.
- **Content:** 50+ tools, fluency exercises, counselling tools, customised step-by-step training plans, statistics and assessments.
- **Community:** group video calls and regular group sessions for real-world speaking practice — genuinely the best feature, and rare.
- **Dosage:** 20–40 minutes daily.
- **Reach:** 50,000+ users across 190+ countries.

**What it gets right:** the community/group practice element, and CBT alongside speech technique.
**Not suitable as-is for a primary school child.**

## Penguin: Stammering Support (BeneTalk)

Positioned as support rather than treatment — a companion app in the STAMMA/UK tradition, oriented toward acceptance and self-management rather than fluency drilling.

| App | Focus | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| DAF Pro | Delayed auditory feedback | Immediate fluency effect, **temporary**. Needs headphones. Listener fatigue. Adults. |

| Speech4Good | Structured practice | Cross-platform, progressive difficulty. Time-heavy. |

## The gaps — what nothing in this list does well

This is the design brief.

1. **Nothing serves the parent and the child as one system.** Child apps gamify practice; tracker apps serve adults self-monitoring. Nothing gives a parent the Lidcombe severity rating workflow **and** gives the child something they want to open, in one place, with one shared history.

2. **Nothing is built around the clinician relationship.** The evidence is unambiguous that these programs work when a clinician supervises. No app produces the thing a clinician actually needs — four weeks of daily severity ratings, practice adherence, and situational notes, exportable, in the format they already read.

3. **Avoidance is untracked.** Every app counts fluency. None track the school age markers that actually predict adult outcomes: hand not raised, word substituted, phone not answered, question not asked. This is the biggest single miss in the category.

4. **Desensitisation is barely represented.** Voluntary stuttering is a core Van Riper technique with an RCT behind it in school age children, and it is almost absent from child-facing apps because it is counter-intuitive to market ("we'll teach your child to stutter on purpose").

5. **School context is invisible.** The classroom is where the bullying happens and where the oral presentations are. No app has a teacher handout, a self-disclosure script to rehearse, or a way to prepare for a specific upcoming speaking situation.

6. **Speech recognition is oversold.** Automatic detection of stuttering moments is not reliably solved. Apps that grade a child's fluency with ASR risk telling a child they failed when they did not. **Self-rating and parent-rating are more honest and more clinically defensible than machine scoring.**

7. **Privacy.** These apps collect recordings of children's voices. Cloud-based processing of a minor's speech is a real consideration.

---

## Design decisions this leads to

For an app built for one child, used alongside a real clinician:

- **Two modes in one app** — kid mode and parent mode, sharing one data store.
- **No cloud, no account, no speech recognition scoring.** Everything stays on the device. Recording is for the child to hear themselves back, not for a machine to grade.
- **Self-rating over machine rating.** The child rates their own attempt. That is itself a therapeutic act — it is Lidcombe's "request self-evaluation" contingency and Van Riper's identification phase.
- **Track avoidance and bravery, not only fluency.** Give the brave-speaking log equal weight with the severity chart.
- **Include voluntary stuttering as a game.** Frame it as a superpower, not a punishment.
- **Build the clinician export in from day one.** CSV plus a printable summary.
- **Say plainly, in the app, that it is not a substitute for a speech pathologist.**
