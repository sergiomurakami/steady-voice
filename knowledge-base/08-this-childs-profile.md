# 8. This child's profile, and what it changes

The single most important input to the design of the app, and to what you should ask a clinician for.

---

## The profile

- Stutters **frequently**
- Bumps in public **and** one-on-one with a parent or carer — roughly the same either way
- **Socially confident.** Talks to people, orders in restaurants, speaks in front of others
- **No fear of speaking.** No avoidance, no word substitution, no hanging back
- **Not ashamed** of stuttering

---

## What that rules out

Most stuttering material for school-age children — including most of the apps, and a good deal of clinical practice — is built around a different child: one who has become anxious about speaking, who avoids, who substitutes words, who is embarrassed. The Michael Palin Centre's school-age programme literally orders its aims as confidence first, anxiety second, fluency third. That ordering is correct for the typical presentation and **wrong for this one.**

So the following are not the work here:

- **Desensitisation / voluntary stuttering.** There is no fear response to reduce. Deliberately stuttering to prove nothing bad happens is pointless for a child who already knows nothing bad happens.
- **Courage ladders and brave-speaking missions.** He already orders his own food and speaks in public.
- **Avoidance tracking.** Nothing to track.
- **Self-disclosure scripts and teasing rehearsal.** Available if wanted, but not a treatment target.
- **Anxiety-focused therapy.** Be sceptical of any clinician who proposes it as the primary approach for this child.

---

## What it points to instead

**The problem is motor, not social.** A child who bumps as much alone with a parent as in front of a class is telling you the driver is speech production, not situational anxiety. That is genuinely useful diagnostic information and worth saying out loud at the first appointment.

The treatment target is therefore **speech production itself** — rhythm, rate, and smooth motor execution — plus the hard part: carrying it out of the practice session into ordinary conversation.

**The best-matched treatment is the Oakville Program** (see §2 and below): the Australian school-age syllable-timed speech program from the ASRC. It is pure motor training, entirely free of emotional content, delivered by the parent under clinician supervision. Its school-age trial ran 6–11 year olds and reported a 77% group mean severity reduction at 12 months.

**Secondary targets**, in order of fit:

1. Prolonged / stretchy speech (speech restructuring, as in Camperdown)
2. Easy onset and light articulatory contact
3. GILCU — building fluent utterance length
4. Pull-outs and cancellations — because when you bump often, getting out of a block cleanly is a high-value skill

---

## Two things worth saying honestly

**1. Low distress is a real advantage, and you should use it.**

The long-term damage from childhood stuttering runs largely through avoidance, shame and social anxiety — the psychosocial pathway in §1. None of that is in play. That means:

- Treatment that would be a hard sell to a self-conscious child is available. Syllable-timed speech sounds odd, and a child who is embarrassed about their speech often refuses it. A child who is unbothered will just do it.
- Transfer practice — deliberately using a technique in real conversation — is the step most programs fail at, and it fails mostly because children are self-conscious about sounding different. This child can actually do it.
- The whole treatment budget can go on the speech rather than being split with emotional work.

**2. It does not mean the stuttering matters less, and it may not stay this way.**

Attitudes toward one's own speech can shift across the school years, and children who stutter are bullied at markedly higher rates than their peers — up to 81% in one review, with the classroom as the main site. That is not a prediction about this child; it is a reason to keep a baseline and keep the door open.

The useful response is *not* to talk a comfortable child into worrying:

- Keep talking about stuttering matter-of-factly, the way you already do.
- Ask occasionally and lightly how school is going for talking.
- Ask the speech pathologist for an **OASES-S** or **CALMS** baseline now. Both capture impact and attitude alongside fluency. A baseline taken while things are fine is worth having, and costs one assessment.
- Act early if anything changes.

---

## What to say at the first appointment

> "He stutters a lot, and it's about the same one-on-one with me at home as it is in public. He's not anxious about it, he's not avoiding anything, and he's not embarrassed — he'll happily order his own food and talk to anyone. So I don't think this is anxiety-driven. Are you trained in the Oakville Program, or syllable-timed speech for school-age children? And can we get an OASES-S or CALMS baseline while he's still comfortable with it, so we'd notice if that changed?"

That paragraph will tell you a great deal about whether the clinician in front of you is the right one.

---

## How the app was tuned to this

See §7 for the full spec. In short:

| Removed | Replaced with |
|---|---|
| Voluntary stuttering exercise | **Syllable Time** — metronome-driven STS, per the Oakville guide |
| Courage-graded brave missions | **Out Loud** — transfer jobs graded by how hard the *situation* is to hold technique in, not by nerve |
| Avoidance event counter | **Situation ratings** — severity by context, to test whether context matters at all |
| Brave-moments counter | **Technique used in real conversation** counter |
| Shame / teasing-centred guides | Oakville explained properly, the motor-vs-anxiety argument, and a light-touch section on keeping an eye on the emotional side |
| — | **Hybrid Session** — the Oakville later-Stage-1 structure, half STS and half natural speech with an adjustable ratio |

The severity scale also defaults to **0–10 (Oakville)** rather than 0–9 (Lidcombe), switchable in settings.
