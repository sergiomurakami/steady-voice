What this app is for

Sometimes words come out bumpy. That is called stuttering, and lots of people have it. It is not your fault, and it is not because you are nervous or not trying.

Your mouth has a very fast job to do. Steady Voice gives it some practice, the same way you would practise a sport or an instrument. You do a little bit, most days, and it gets easier.

Three true things

  • Bumpy days and smooth days both happen. A bumpy day does not mean you did anything wrong.
  • Nobody in this app is listening to you or giving you a score. You decide how it went.
  • This app helps, but it is not a speech pathologist. It works best when you have a real one too.

Your side · tab 1

Today

This is the page you land on. It tells you what to do today, and nothing else. If you only ever use this page, that is completely fine.

The number in the corner

That is your streak — how many days in a row you have practised. It does not go back to zero just because you have not started yet today. Practise once and it goes up by one.

Three jobs

Underneath, there are three practice jobs picked for today. Tap one and it opens.

When you finish one, a tick appears next to it. Three ticks is a great day. One tick is still a good day.

Today's out loud job

One thing to try in real life, not on the screen. Something like use syllable talking for two sentences while we make dinner. There is a whole page about these later.

Why three, and why these three? They change every day so you do not get bored, and the first one is always a rhythm exercise, because that is the one doing the most work.

How long should I do?

Short and often beats long and rare. A few goes of five or ten minutes across the day is much better than one big go once a week. Speech pathologists ask for four to six short goes a day at the start.

You do not have to do all of that on the app. Talking to a grown-up in syllable talking while you walk the dog counts too.

Pick Syllable Time. It is the one this whole app is built around, and it comes from a real treatment programme used by speech pathologists in Australia. The other eight are helpers.

Every exercise has the same shape

  • Something to say, in big letters in the middle.
  • A line underneath telling you how to say it.
  • Buttons to hear it, record yourself, and play it back.

Rhythm and rate

Syllable Time

The main one. You say every syllable in time with a beat, like a drum keeping you steady.

One syllable lights up on each beat. Say that syllable.

What a syllable is

A syllable is one beat of a word. Cat has one. Chil-dren has two. Tram-po-line has three. The app puts little dashes between them so you can see where they split.

How to do it

  1. Press Start beat. You will hear a click and see the dots light up.
  2. Say one syllable on each beat. The highlighted one is where you should be.
  3. Make every syllable the same length. None longer, none louder.
  4. Join them up smoothly, like singing a line. Do not chop them apart.
  5. Keep your normal voice — normal loudness, normal expression. It should still sound like you.
The most common mistake. Making it sound chopped up and robotic. It should flow. If it sounds like a robot, the beat is too fast — slide it slower.

The buttons

▶ Start beatStarts and stops the metronome. Press again to stop.
🔊 Click onTurns the clicking sound on or off. The dots still flash either way.
slower ⟷ fasterStart slow. Only speed up when slow feels completely easy. The app remembers your speed for next time.
Longer ones →Swaps between short sentences and longer ones. Start short.

Rhythm and rate

Hybrid Session

Half syllable talking, half your normal voice. This is the bridge between practising and real life.

First half · syllable talkingSecond half · normal talking

Set how long the whole thing runs, press start, and do the first half in syllable talking with a grown-up. When the timer switches over, just talk normally about whatever you like. You will usually find the smoothness carries over from the first half into the second. That is the whole point.

The top slider

How much of the time is syllable talking. Start at half and half.

The bottom slider

How long the whole session runs, from 6 to 20 minutes.

Over the weeks, a grown-up slides the top one further towards normal talking — half, then more, then more again. The goal is that one day you do not need the syllable half at all.

Stretchy Speech

A wave moves across the screen. You talk along with it, stretching your sounds out.

The orange dot rides the wave. Match its speed.

Stretch the vowels — the aaa and ooo sounds — and ease gently into every word. Nobody talks like this all day; it is a practice speed. Being told "slow down" never works, but having something to match your speed to does.

Smooth mechanics

Three ways to make sounds easier

These are about what your mouth and breath are actually doing.

Gentle Starts

Words that start with a vowel — a, e, i, o, u

Begin the word softly, letting the sound fade in, instead of punching it out. Think of a car rolling forward rather than a ball being kicked.

You practise on words like apple, open, every and ocean, because there is no hard consonant at the front to press on.

Feather Touch

The pressy sounds — p, b, t, d, k, g

These are the ones where lips and tongue press together, and pressure can build up until nothing comes out at all.

So touch lightly. A feather landing, not a hammer. Practise on pizza, table, dog, cake, bike.

Wave Breathing

Breath, and where talking happens

A circle grows and shrinks. Breathe in through your nose while it grows, hold for a moment, then talk while it shrinks.

Here is the thing worth knowing: talking happens on the way out, never on the way in. If you have run out of air, the word cannot come. Press Start breathing and follow the circle.

You do not need to think about all three at once. Ever. Pick one, do it for five minutes, forget about it. Your mouth learns them one at a time.

Building up

Starting small and growing

Word Ladder

One word, then two, then more

You start at the bottom rung with a single word — Pizza. Say it smoothly and you climb: I like pizza. I like hot pizza. I like hot cheese pizza. Six rungs to the top.

If a rung comes out bumpy, tap Bumpy and you drop back one and try again. You cannot fail this one. It just finds the rung where your talking is comfortable, and then nudges upward from there.

Stepping Stones

Pausing in the right places

A long sentence is a lot to carry all at once. So the app puts little marks in it, like this:

After school today / I want to ride my bike / down to the park.

Take a small pause at each mark. The pauses are yours. Nobody is timing you, and a pause is not a mistake — everyone pauses when they talk.

When a word gets stuck

Rescue Moves

Three things to do in the moment

Slide out. When you are stuck on a word, do not stop and do not push harder. Ease out of the middle of it and s-l-i-d-e to the end. Being stuck is not the end of the word.

Say it again, easy. If a word came out bumpy, finish it. Stop for one second. Then say the same word again, gently. You are not fixing a mistake — you are showing your mouth the easy way is available.

Get set first. When you can feel a hard word coming, get your mouth ready before you start. You usually know a second beforehand. That second is enough.

Practise these when you are calm, not only when you are stuck. That way they are already there when you need them.

Your side · tab 3

Read

Reading out loud, in four sizes: single words, short phrases, sentences, then short stories.

The trick that really works

Read it at the same time as a grown-up, out loud, both voices together. Almost everyone who stutters is smooth when reading in unison with someone. Do that first, then read it again on your own. It is also the single best thing to ask a teacher for at school.

Tap the four buttons at the top to change level. Next and Back move through them. Try a pass in syllable talking too.

Your side · tab 5

Talk

Ten minutes of just talking with a grown-up, somewhere quiet.

There is a question card to get you started, and a timer. Press Another question if you do not like the one showing. This is not a test — it is the ordinary conversation all the practice is aiming at.

What the grown-up should be doing

  • Stop what you are doing. Turn and look at them.
  • Slow your own talking down and pause more. Show it, do not say it.
  • Wait. Let them finish the word themselves.
  • Answer what they said, not how they said it.
  • No corrections here. This one is just talking.
Ten minutes counts as practice. When the timer runs out the app adds it to the day automatically — you do not have to press anything else.

What an ordinary day looks like

You do not have to do everything. Here is a day that would be plenty.

The back half

For the grown-up

Press the two-people icon at the top right. Four tabs: Today, Progress, Guides, Find help. Same plain language, still nothing complicated.

Today — the daily rating

One number for the whole day, reflecting typical speech. Not the worst moment, not a single sample.

012345678910
0 · no stuttering10 · extremely severe

This is the measure Australian speech pathologists actually ask parents to keep. The app defaults to the 0–10 scale used by the Oakville Program; you can switch it to the 0–9 Lidcombe scale in Find help → Settings to match whichever your clinician uses.

Making the number mean something

  • In the first week, write down what a 2 day and a 6 day look like in your house. Then rate against those, every time.
  • Rate the speech, not the day. A rough day at school pulls the number up unfairly.
  • Rate after they are in bed. If the number becomes a score your child is graded on, you have invented a pressure that was not there.
  • Your clinician will want your rating within one point of theirs. Later on, when severity is low, they will want them identical.

Where were the bumps?

Optional, and the most useful thing in the app for your situation. Rate only the situations that actually came up — leave the rest blank. Over a few weeks this answers the question a single daily number cannot: does context change anything?

If the bars come out roughly level, that confirms the picture and simplifies the plan. If one stands well clear, that is worth naming to the clinician.

Grown-up side · tab 2

Progress

Last 28 days. Read the direction, not the individual days. Stuttering is naturally variable — a bad day after a good week is normal, not relapse.

For your appointment

Export CSV saves a spreadsheet of everything — daily ratings, minutes, real-world use, situation scores, notes. Print gives you the page on paper.

Take it in. Four weeks of daily ratings gives a clinician far more signal than half an hour observing a child in an unfamiliar room — and the Oakville guide notes that a child's speech in clinic often does not match their speech at home.

Grown-up side · tabs 3 and 4

Guides, and Find help

Guides holds the short version of the research — the treatment programmes explained, what to ask a school for, how to rate consistently, and how to pay for therapy in Australia. Tap a heading to open it.

Find help holds the clinic list, phone numbers, and the settings.

The three prompting rules

These matter more than anything else in this manual, so they get their own box.

1. About once an hour, at most. Outside practice sessions, a nudge to use the technique should be rare.

2. Only where they are comfortable. At home, with family. Not in front of their friends.

3. Never right after a bump. A prompt that lands immediately after a stutter turns the technique into a telling-off, and the child will quietly stop using it. Prompt during a smooth stretch instead, or model it yourself and let them join in.

Things not to say

Avoid

  • "Slow down."
  • "Relax." "Take a breath."
  • "Think about it first."
  • Finishing their word.
  • "Start again" in front of others.

Instead

  • Slow your own speech and pause more.
  • Stop, turn, and look at them.
  • Wait. Let them land the word.
  • Answer what they said.
  • Praise smooth talking far more often than you mention bumps.
One more, from the treatment guide. If you are not certain a disfluency was a real stutter, do not respond to it at all. Uncertain corrections do more harm than no correction.

Where the data lives

QuestionAnswer
Where is it stored?In the browser, on that one device. Nowhere else.
Is anything uploaded?No. No account, no server, no analytics. The app makes no network calls at all once it has loaded.
Does it listen to my child?No. There is no speech recognition anywhere in it. Recordings are saved on the device so you can compare progress and play one to your speech pathologist, and they are never uploaded — the app has no server to upload them to. Delete any of them, or all of them, under Recordings; they also go with Erase all data.
How do I back it up?Export CSV on the Progress tab. That file is the backup.
What if I clear my browser?The data goes with it. Export first.
Two devices?They will not share. Pick one device and keep the ratings there.

Settings, in Find help

  • Child's first name — used only for the greeting.
  • Severity rating scale — 0–10 (Oakville) or 0–9 (Lidcombe). Existing ratings are not rescaled, so pick one early and stay with it.
  • Erase all data — permanent. It asks first.

Installing it properly

Open the app in a browser on a phone or tablet, then use Add to Home Screen. It gets its own icon and opens full screen, like an ordinary app. It works without internet once it has loaded.

The one thing to remember

Steady Voice is practice scaffolding, not treatment. There is no good evidence that any app treats stuttering on its own, and the Oakville treatment guide says plainly that the programme is not designed to be run by a parent without a clinician. Use this alongside a speech pathologist, not instead of one.
